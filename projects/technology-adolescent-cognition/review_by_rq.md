> How does technology influence cognitive processes relevant for learning?

**Evidence found in 19/20 sources (95%)**

### Summary of Findings

**Alkal (2025) [Source 1]**

The study found that smartphone addiction (SA) significantly and negatively predicts psychological flexibility (PF) and emotion regulation (ER) over time. Specifically, higher levels of smartphone addiction led to decreases in adolescents' psychological flexibility (which includes attention to the present moment and cognitive defusion) and their ability to regulate emotions.
*Effect size: β = -0.39 (SA to PF), β = -0.23 (SA to ER)*

> The findings indicated that SA at T1 negatively predicted PW at T3 (β=-0.34, p<.001), PF at T2 (β=-0.39, p<.001), and ER at T2 (β=-0.23, p<.001).

**Chen et al. (2024) [Source 2]**

The study found that exposure to competitive video games negatively influences cognitive processes by increasing impulsivity and decreasing executive functioning (self-control). This effect was observed as both a short-term outcome in an experimental setting and a long-term developmental trajectory in a longitudinal study.
*Effect size: β = 0.28 (Study 1); β = 0.03 (Study 2)*

> playing competitive video games can lead to a decrease in executive functioning and self-control, and a lack of control over impulsive responses

**Deng et al. (2023) [Source 4]**

The study found that educational computer games significantly improved academic self-regulation (involving cognitive and metacognitive strategies) and academic resilience (described as a cognitive variable).
*Effect size: Partial eta squared = 0.370 (Academic self-regulation); Partial eta squared = 0.538 (Academic resilience)*

> Academic resilience is one of the cognitive (individual) variables affecting academic achievement, which has been one of the academic empowerments and has the greatest impact on academic success

**Gerosa & Gui (2023) [Source 5]**

The study found an overall null effect of early smartphone acquisition (at age 10-11) on language proficiency trajectories compared to late acquisition (age 12+). However, for students who were already heavy screen media users (TV/video games > 2 hours/day) before middle school, early smartphone acquisition significantly negatively impacted language proficiency trends.
*Effect size: β = -0.222 (interaction); CATT = -0.246 (heavy users)*

> Results indicate an overall null effect of smartphone early owning on adolescents’ language proficiency trajectories, while a negative effect is found on those who were already heavy screen media users before receiving the device.
> - Page 1

**He et al. (2023) [Source 6]**

The study found that increased social media use was associated with reduced volume in subcortical structures relevant to cognitive processes, specifically the hippocampus (involved in memory), caudate, and thalamus. However, screen media use was not significantly associated with behavioral measures of attention or thought problems after correcting for multiple comparisons.
*Effect size: b = -0.010 (hippocampus volume)*

> In addition, more social media use was associated with a reduced volume of the hippocampus, caudate, and thalamus proper.

**León Méndez et al. (2024) [Source 9]**

The systematic review found that Internet Addiction (IA) and Smartphone Addiction (SPA) are associated with impairments in cognitive control and executive functions, including inhibitory control, decision-making, and working memory. These behavioral issues correspond to functional abnormalities in brain regions such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and parietal lobes.

> Included studies (n = 21) revealed that both behavioral addictions were associated with impairments in cognitive control related to reward processing (ACC, insula, amygdala) and executive function (DLPFC, frontal, parietal lobes) in adolescents and young adults.
> - Page 1

**Maeneja et al. (2025) [Source 10]**

The review found that excessive exposure to Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs) generally negatively affects executive functions, specifically working memory, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and attention. However, the impact varies by content type, with educational content showing potential positive effects or fewer difficulties compared to entertainment content.

> Most studies indicated that excessive ICT exposure negatively affects executive functions, particularly working memory, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and attention.
> - Page 1 (Abstract)

**Malakul & Park (2023) [Source 11]**

The study examined the effect of AI-generated auto-subtitles on learning comprehension and cognitive load. Results showed that students using auto-subtitles had significantly higher learning comprehension scores compared to the no-subtitles group, though not significantly different from the edited subtitles group. Regarding cognitive load, there was no statistically significant difference found among the three conditions (auto-subtitles, edited subtitles, no subtitles).
*Effect size: η²=0.10*

> The results of the quantitative analysis showed that students using the auto-subtitles systems had higher mean scores for learning comprehension on how well they could remember the content of videos than those in the no-subtitles group.
> - Page 10

**Romero et al. (2025) [Source 13]**

The study found a consistent negative association between media multitasking reading habits (MMRH) and text comprehension across three experiments, regardless of reading medium (print vs. screen), text difficulty, or the presence of a distraction task. MMRH was also negatively associated with metacomprehension (accuracy of performance prediction), with this relationship being more pronounced when reading long texts on screens in Experiment 1.
*Effect size: estimate: − 0.20 (Exp 1 text comp); estimate: − 0.15 (Exp 2 text comp); estimate: − 0.13 (Exp 3 text comp)*

> Main results showed a negative association between media multitasking habits and text comprehension across all the experiments, even after controlling for students' sustained attention and short-term verbal memory capacity, reading comprehension skills, and prior knowledge.

**Shan et al. (2025) [Source 15]**

The study found that media multitasking negatively predicts cognitive processes, specifically executive functioning and divergent thinking, over a six-month period. High levels of media multitasking were associated with reduced executive function.
*Effect size: β = -0.38 (MMT to Executive Function); β = -0.11 (MMT to Divergent Thinking in regression)*

> T1 media multitasking significantly negatively predicted executive functioning at T2 (β=−0.38, p<0.001), and executive functioning at T2 significantly negatively predicted divergent thinking at T2 (β=− 0.18, p=0.010).

**Stevic et al. (2024) [Source 16]**

The study found that children's nighttime smartphone use was positively associated with parent-reported attentional problems over time. Specifically, smartphone use at Time 1 predicted higher levels of attentional problems at Time 2, which in turn negatively predicted school performance.
*Effect size: β = 0.11*

> In line with our first hypothesis, we found that nighttime smartphone use at T1 was significantly associated with parent-reported children’s attentional problems at T2, b = 0.09, SE = 0.04, β = 0.11, p = .034.

**Thorell et al. (2024) [Source 17]**

The systematic review found that a majority (74%) of longitudinal studies showed significant associations between digital media use and later ADHD symptoms (inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity), although effect sizes were generally small. The review discusses the 'scan and shift hypothesis,' suggesting that the fast pace of digital media may deplete attentional resources, making sustained attention difficult.
*Effect size: correlation coefficients < 0.30*

> The present results showed that a majority (74%) of the studies found significant associations between digital media and later ADHD symptom levels. Effects sizes were often relatively small (e.g., correlation coefficients < 0.30).
> - Page 2519

**Turan et al. (2024) [Source 18]**

The study found that male adolescents with Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) exhibited significantly poorer executive functions compared to healthy controls. Specifically, the IGD group showed large deficits in behavioral regulation (inhibition, set-shifting, emotional control) and metacognition (initiation, working memory, planning-organization, monitoring).
*Effect size: Cohen's d = 1.0–2.0*

> The effect size of the impairments in executive functions and self-regulation skills was large (Cohen’s d=1.0–2.0).
> - Page 1

**Wallace et al. (2023) [Source 19]**

The study found that social media use, television viewing, and video gaming were associated with ADHD symptoms and impulsivity via neuropsychological functions. Specifically, social media use was longitudinally linked to ADHD symptoms through a pathway involving disrupted inhibitory control (response inhibition) and impulsive temperament. Television and video gaming were associated with deficits in response inhibition and working memory at the between-person level (common vulnerability), but not longitudinally within-persons.
*Effect size: Mediation between-person estimate = 0.484 (social media -> impulsivity -> ADHD); Mediation between-person estimate = 0.029 (social media -> response inhibition -> ADHD)*

> Results showed that the common vulnerability to greater social media use, television viewing, and video gaming and ADHD behaviors was mediated by neurophysiological functions and impulsivity at between-person levels.

## RQ1.1: To what extent does frequent digital media multitasking a...

> To what extent does frequent digital media multitasking and smartphone use impact adolescents' attention spans and executive functions (e.g., working memory, inhibitory control) in learning contexts?

**Evidence found in 12/20 sources (60%)**

### Summary of Findings

**Choe & Yu (2024) [Source 3]**

The study examined the impact of various computer and mobile phone activities on attention deficit (defined as difficulties focusing on schoolwork and completing tasks). It found that online gaming, watching videos, and accessing adult websites were associated with higher initial attention deficit. Conversely, searching for information for educational purposes and listening to music were associated with lower initial attention deficit. The study did not explicitly assess media multitasking.
*Effect size: B ranges from -0.063 to 0.069*

> Adolescents using more online gaming presented higher initial attention deficit (B=0.067, p<0.001) compared to those who played online games less.
> - Page 8

**He et al. (2023) [Source 6]**

The study examined the longitudinal association between various types of screen media use (passive, video games, social media) and attention problems (measured via the Child Behavior Checklist). It found no significant associations between any type of screen media use and attention problems after correcting for multiple comparisons.
*Effect size: b = .024 (Total Screen Time on Attention, ns)*

> However, social media use was not associated with any mental health outcomes.
> - Page 6

**Howard et al. (2025) [Source 7]**

The study found a small negative association between self-regulation and media multitasking, and a very large negative association between self-regulation and problematic mobile phone use. The association with the amount of mobile phone use was small and non-significant when adjusted.
*Effect size: r = -.17 (multitasking); r = -.41 (problematic mobile phone use)*

> Self-regulation had a small negative association with media multitasking (r = −.17, p = .014, R2 = .03), such that children or adolescents with lower self-regulation engaged in more media multitasking with other non-media activities
> - Page 23

**Kong et al. (2023) [Source 8]**

The study indicates that frequent media multitasking is negatively associated with executive functions (inhibitory control and working memory) in adolescents. A moderator analysis found no significant difference in this relationship between adolescents and young adults, suggesting the negative impact applies to adolescents as well.
*Effect size: Inhibitory control d = -0.307; Working memory d = -0.243*

> Results showed that the negative relationship between media multitasking and cognitive control was similar for adolescents and young adults.
> - Page 26

**León Méndez et al. (2024) [Source 9]**

In adolescents, Internet Addiction was linked to reduced functional connectivity in fronto-parietal circuits and the medial prefrontal cortex, as well as ineffective connectivity in pathways related to response inhibition. Young adults with IA showed reduced top-down control of attention and executive control.

> Adolescents with high IA scores have been found to have reduced FC in cortico-subcortical circuits (prefrontal and parietal)... in the right fronto-parietal circuit, mPFC and anterior DMN connectivity compared to controls.
> - Page 8

**Maeneja et al. (2025) [Source 10]**

The review links adverse effects on executive functions to multitasking and increased screen time on devices including smartphones, though the evidence for adolescents is limited to early adolescence (up to 12 years). Specifically, excessive use of smartphones and other devices is associated with impaired cognitive flexibility, and multitasking is identified as a factor linked to adverse effects.

> Adverse effects were linked to increased screen time, poor sleep quality, and multitasking.
> - Page 1 (Abstract)

**Romero et al. (2025) [Source 13]**

In the experiment involving adolescents (secondary-school students), media multitasking reading habits were not significantly correlated with sustained attention (r = -0.02). However, these habits were significantly negatively associated with text comprehension performance, even after controlling for sustained attention.
*Effect size: r = -0.02 (correlation between MMRH and sustained attention in adolescents)*

> Specifically, the higher the frequency of multitasking reading in daily life contexts, the poorer the text comprehension scores in our experiment, regardless of students' reading comprehension skills and sustained attention
> - Page 11

**Shan et al. (2025) [Source 15]**

Frequent media multitasking was found to significantly and negatively predict executive functioning (measured as inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory) over time. The authors suggest this behavior leads to reduced attention, concentration, and working memory.
*Effect size: β = -0.38*

> T1 media multitasking significantly negatively predicted executive functioning at T2 (β=−0.38, p<0.001)
> - Page 6

**Stevic et al. (2024) [Source 16]**

The study provides evidence that nighttime smartphone use is a predictor of increased attentional problems in adolescents over time. While it did not explicitly measure multitasking, it found that active engagement (checking, texting, social media) at night led to greater parent-reported inattentiveness.
*Effect size: β = 0.11*

> The findings revealed that children’s nighttime smartphone use was positively related to parent-reported perceptions of children’s attentional problems over time
> - p. 223

**Thorell et al. (2024) [Source 17]**

The review includes evidence that media multitasking is associated with later inattention, particularly in younger adolescents and girls. High mobile phone use was also linked to an increased risk of ADHD symptoms. However, the review explicitly notes a lack of longitudinal studies investigating specific executive functions (like working memory or inhibitory control) as mediators.
*Effect size: OR = 1.10 to 3.36 (mobile phones); b = 0.16-0.43 (multitasking)*

> Baumgartner et al. [47] found a significant association between screen time for media multitasking (i.e., using several different media simultaneously) and later ADHD symptom levels in between-subject but not in within-subject analyses.
> - Page 2516

**Wallace et al. (2023) [Source 19]**

The study provides evidence that social media use is associated with deficits in inhibitory control (response inhibition) and that television and video gaming are associated with deficits in both response inhibition and working memory. Specifically, increased social media use within a given year predicted concurrent difficulties in neurocognitive inhibitory control, which in turn predicted impulsive behaviors and ADHD symptoms. Working memory deficits mediated the relationship between TV/video games and ADHD symptoms only at the between-person level.
*Effect size: Within-person estimate = 0.001 (Social media -> Response inhibition on impulsivity -> ADHD)*

> As shown in Fig. 2, increased time spent in front of social media leads to neurocognitive inhibitory control difficulties, which in turn result in a concurrent increase in self-reported impulsive behaviors and a consequent increase in ADHD symptoms in the same year, as well as the next year.
> - Page 9

**Xu et al. (2025) [Source 20]**

The study indicates that problematic smartphone use (mobile phone involvement) is a stronger predictor of deficits in executive functions (specifically impulsivity) than phone-checking frequency. Problematic mobile phone use predicted higher impulsivity in both females and males. Problematic social media use predicted attention problems in males.
*Effect size: B = 0.219 (female), B = 0.149 (male) for mobile phone use to impulsivity*

> Problematic mobile phone use was associated with heightened impulsivity (A3→UPPS, B = 0.219)... and diminished school involvement
> - Page 731
